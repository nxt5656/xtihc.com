# Why Are Some People in China So Afraid of “Winning”? (Zhihu Replies)

The following are translations of eight replies to the Zhihu question “Why are some people in China so afraid of ‘winning’?”, presented in the order of the supplied screenshots. Author names and publication or edit times follow the screenshots; missing times have not been filled in. No links to the question or individual answers were provided.

The replies distinguish actual wins from 赢学, rendered here as “triumphalism”: declaring victory regardless of whether ordinary lives improve.

Zhihu user Johnsp, published January 24, 2026, at 10:40:

> I once posted on Tieba saying I really wanted two days off a week, that I thought my working hours were too long and my pay too low
>
> Then a whole bunch of “winners” came along to tell me I was living better than people in the United States, Japan and South Korea, and that I'd already won
>
> I couldn't care less whether I've won or not. I just want to live a little better. If I get two days off, I'll hope to get three someday. If I'm making five thousand a month now, I'll hope it can rise to six thousand someday
>
> But whenever I hope life will get better in the future, someone always pops up to tell me I'm already living very well and shouldn't keep imagining a better life
>
> I don't understand

Zhihu user 香坊大呲花, edited February 20, 2026, at 19:21:

> It's simple: people aren't afraid of winning. They're afraid of triumphalism.
>
> What's the difference? Here's a small example.
>
> Your work schedule goes from 996 to 965. That's winning.
>
> You work 996, but someone tells you even 996 is better than dropping below the “execution line.” That's triumphalism.
>
> The difference is that winning can make your life better, while triumphalism won't. Some people will even try to shut you up when you complain.
>
> This is a real example I saw under a question about the “execution line.”

“996” means working from 9 a.m. to 9 p.m., six days a week; “965” means 9 a.m. to 6 p.m., five days a week. The “execution line” (斩杀线) is used here as a metaphor for the point at which someone's livelihood collapses.
